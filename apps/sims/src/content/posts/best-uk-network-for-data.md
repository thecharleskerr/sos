The best UK network for data is the one with signal where you spend your day, and choosing on reputation instead of postcode means paying every month for data you cannot use. Only four networks own masts in the UK: EE, O2, Three and Vodafone. Every other brand rents one of those four. So the choice has two parts. Pick the host with good coverage at your home and your workplace, then buy the cheapest plan on that host with the allowance you actually use.

This takes about ten minutes. Every figure below comes from Ofcom or the network's own pages, with the date we checked it. The deal cards above are re-checked every Monday.

## Which networks own their masts, and who runs on them?

Four networks own their radio masts and everyone else rents space on them. Our network table records the host for each brand we list. SMARTY and iD Mobile run on Three. VOXI and Lebara run on Vodafone. giffgaff runs on O2. Coverage comes from the host, not the brand, so two brands on the same host see the same signal at your postcode.

One change matters here. Vodafone and Three merged in 2025 and now trade as VodafoneThree. Vodafone's newsroom says that where a mast has been upgraded for sharing, a phone with either SIM connects to whichever of the two has the best signal at that spot, and that its own brands get the same at no extra charge. The core networks stay separate. Ofcom's checker still shows Vodafone and Three as two networks, so check both.

## How do I check coverage at my own postcode?

Use Ofcom's Map Your Mobile checker, which covers all four host networks. Enter your postcode and Ofcom shows two things. The first is coverage: whether a 4G or 5G signal is predicted indoors and outdoors, and whether it is likely to be good or variable. The second is performance: how likely you are to be able to stream video in your postal district. Ofcom's update page says the data is now given in 50 metre squares where it holds that detail.

Ofcom is clear that these are predictions. Thick walls, trees and nearby buildings can all change what you actually get. So treat the checker as a shortlist tool and confirm it with the host's own checker. EE, O2, Three and Vodafone each publish one. EE, O2 and Three say on those pages that the results are a guide rather than a guarantee.

## Here is exactly what to do

1. Open Ofcom's Map Your Mobile checker and enter your home postcode. Note which of EE, O2, Three and Vodafone show good indoor 4G or 5G. Repeat for your workplace or anywhere else you spend hours each week.
2. Run the same postcodes through the host's own coverage checker. If Ofcom and the network agree, you have your host. If they disagree, pick a plan you can leave, such as a one month rolling SIM, and test it.
3. Check how much data you used in each of the last three months. Your current provider's app or bill shows this. If you never go near your allowance, you do not need unlimited. [Our guide on whether unlimited is worth it](/blog/is-unlimited-data-worth-it/) walks through the arithmetic.
4. Pick the cheapest brand on your host with that allowance. 5. Switch and keep your number. Ofcom's rules let you text PAC to 65075. Your provider must reply within a minute with a code that is valid for 30 days. ## Is unlimited data really unlimited?

Only on the networks that say so in their own terms, and each one says something different. Three's support page says an Unlimited plan has no data caps and no fair usage policy, without capping speeds. SMARTY's plan page says no speed restrictions and no fair use policies in the UK, with tethering included. iD Mobile's unlimited page says unlimited really means unlimited, with no speed caps or data limits for domestic use.

The other networks set conditions. O2's unlimited tariff terms say it may investigate if you regularly tether to 12 or more devices or use 650GB twice in a six month period, and that it can apply traffic shaping or throttling. giffgaff, which runs on O2, gives the same figures in its help centre. EE's unlimited terms say use above 600GB a month is treated as non-personal, and EE can deprioritise your traffic at busy times or move you to a business plan. Vodafone sells three tiers: Unlimited Lite with a maximum speed of 2Mbps, Unlimited at 10Mbps and Unlimited Max at full speed, so the word unlimited on its own does not tell you the speed. Lebara's fair usage policy says there is no data cap where unlimited is included, subject to fraud exceptions. VOXI's help page says its unlimited social, music and video apps only work while you have some general data left.

All of these are UK terms. Every network in the table caps data in the EU, from 5GB at giffgaff to 50GB at EE. If you travel, read [our EU roaming guide](/blog/best-sim-only-deal-for-eu-roaming/) before you buy.

## Isn't one network simply faster than the rest?

Ofcom's Mobile Matters research, built from crowdsourced data, found Three had the shortest file download times over 5G, followed by Vodafone, while EE was fastest over 4G and downloads took longer on O2 over both. Those are national averages. They tell you nothing about the mast nearest your kitchen. A network that is fastest on average and weak at your postcode is the wrong network for you. Postcode first, then averages. If you are wondering whether 5G matters for you at all, [our 5G guide](/blog/do-i-need-5g/) covers it.

## Is a cheaper brand on the same masts worse than the network itself?

The coverage is the same, because it comes from the same masts. What differs is the plan: the roaming cap, the price rise, whether 5G is included, and any perks. iD Mobile's roaming cap is 30GB where giffgaff's is 5GB, on different hosts, and the table above shows the spread. The deal card shows each plan's rise in pounds and pence, or No price rise, so you can compare a brand against its host on the only figures that change. ## Will the price go up during my contract?

Sometimes, and the card tells you by how much before you click. A contract can carry a rise that lands in a fixed month each year, so a plan bought now may cost more before it ends. The card shows the rise in pounds and pence, or "No price rise", along with the total cost of the contract.
