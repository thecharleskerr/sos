Choosing a phone contract when SIM only plus a phone bought outright would have cost less wastes money every month for the whole term, and choosing SIM only when a network's subsidy on the phone beats that saving costs you the same way in reverse. The only way to know which side wins is to add up the total cost of each option over the same number of months. That is not a guess, it is a sum, and it takes about ten minutes.

Every figure below comes from a network's own pages or from UK law, dated, so you can run the sum with real numbers rather than a rule of thumb. Deals on this site are re-checked every Monday, so the SIM only side of the comparison stays current while you decide.

## The total cost method in one sum

Work out two totals over the same number of months, then compare them. For a contract, the total is the monthly price times the number of months, plus any upfront cost the network states. For SIM only plus a phone, the total is the phone's price, paid once, plus a SIM only plan's monthly price times the same number of months.

If you are buying a new phone, its own maker states the price on its own site, apple.com, samsung.com or store.google.com, and you should note the date you checked it, since prices on those pages change. If you are keeping the phone you already have, that side of the sum is zero. Either way, take the SIM only monthly price from a live card rather than a figure you remember or a screenshot from last month. This week's cheapest pick above already has its total contract cost worked out for you, so you only need to multiply it by however many months you plan to compare. Our sister site, [Save on Smartphones](https://saveonsmartphones.co.uk/), runs the same total cost method against handset contracts, including its guide on an [iPhone contract against buying outright](https://saveonsmartphones.co.uk/blog/iphone-18-contract-vs-buying-outright/), if a phone contract is the one you are actually weighing up.

## What the law now says the headline price must include

The headline price has to be the whole price, not the first instalment of it. Since the Digital Markets, Competition and Consumers Act 2024 came into force, GOV.UK's guidance on unfair commercial practices sets out that a trader must give the customer either the total price of the product, including any mandatory fees, taxes, charges or other payments the consumer must pay to purchase it, or, where that cannot be calculated in advance, how it will be calculated. GOV.UK's summary guidance adds that a charge counts as mandatory if the customer must pay it to buy the product at all.

This is the rule against drip pricing: showing you a low figure first, then adding mandatory charges once you are further into the purchase. The Act treats that as unlawful, and GOV.UK's guidance notes a trader who breaks it risks a fine of up to ten per cent of turnover or three hundred thousand pounds, whichever is greater. That is why every deal card on this site states its total contract cost alongside the monthly price rather than the monthly price alone. It does not do your sum for you when you are weighing a contract against SIM only plus a phone bought separately, since that is two different purchases, but it does mean the contract figure you are given should already be complete.

## Why EE and O2 show two monthly charges, not one

A phone contract's monthly bill is often two payments stitched together, and both need to go into your total. EE's own help pages describe Flex Pay as separating the cost of the handset from the airtime plan, so you have a device credit agreement running alongside a rolling airtime plan, each billed on its own schedule. O2's custom plans pages describe the same split by name: the Device Plan is the cost of your phone or tablet, and the Airtime Plan is the cost of your data, minutes and texts.

For the total cost sum, that split matters less than it sounds, since you are adding the monthly price to itself either way. What it changes is what happens later. O2's pages describe an auto-save feature that lowers your bill once the device part is paid off, so you stop paying for a phone you already own. EE's structure means the airtime plan carries on as its own rolling contract once the device credit agreement ends, whether or not you upgrade. Either way, the total you should compare against SIM only plus a phone is the full monthly figure for the whole term, not just the part that is left once a device is paid off.

## Here is exactly what to do

1. Pick the number of months you want to compare, usually the length of the contract you have been quoted or the SIM only card above.
2. Read the total contract cost off the SIM only card above. It already includes any upfront cost, so this is your SIM only side of the sum.
3. If you need a new phone, note its official price from apple.com, samsung.com or store.google.com, with today's date. If you are keeping your phone, this figure is zero.
4. Add the phone's price to the SIM only total from step two. That is your SIM only plus phone total.
5. For the contract you are weighing it against, multiply its stated monthly price by its term in months, then add any upfront cost the network states. Under the DMCC Act, that figure should already be the full price with nothing added later.
6. Compare the two totals over the same number of months. Whichever is lower is cheaper for you, on the figures you used, this week.

## Will a contract or SIM only show up on a credit check?

It depends on the network, and the two are not the same. O2's own eligibility checker page says its initial check is not a credit check, so your credit rating is not affected there, but that a full credit assessment follows later in the checkout journey before a Pay Monthly purchase completes. giffgaff's help pages state plainly that it will not run a credit check before you join on a SIM only contract. SMARTY publishes a page titled no credit check SIM only deals. If avoiding a search on your file matters to you, going SIM only on a network that states no credit check is the more certain route, on their own wording, than a phone contract that carries a device finance agreement.

## What if I want to keep the phone for three years?

Then the sum still applies, it just runs for longer. A contract locks its monthly price and its device subsidy for the term the network states, so the total cost method above works the same way over three years as it does over one, provided you use the actual term. SIM only has an advantage here too: Ofcom's rule from December 2021 stops UK mobile companies selling locked handsets, so a phone bought since then should already accept a SIM from any network, and keeping it for years on a SIM only plan means you are never paying twice for a device you already own outright.

## Doesn't a contract get me the latest phone through an upgrade?

Only by restarting the sum, not by skipping it. EE's Flex Pay structure treats the device as its own credit agreement, so moving to a new phone before that agreement ends generally means settling what is left of it first. O2's pages describe the same idea from the other side: once the device part of a custom plan is paid off, your bill drops rather than rolling straight into a new one. An upgrade offered mid-contract is really a new device agreement layered on top of the old one's remaining balance, so the total cost method above is exactly what you should run again at that point, comparing the new contract's full total against SIM only plus buying the new phone outright.

## When does a contract actually beat SIM only?

When you have no cash to put down for the phone and the network's subsidy on the device is bigger than the saving SIM only plus buying outright would have given you, which the sum above is the only way to know for certain. A strong subsidy on an expensive phone, spread interest free over a long term, can beat paying for that phone outright even against a cheap SIM only plan. A weak subsidy on a phone you could buy outright for a modest price usually loses. There is no shortcut past running both totals for your own case.
