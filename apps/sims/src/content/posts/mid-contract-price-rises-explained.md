A mid-contract price rise is a monthly amount you agreed to before you knew what it would be, and not checking it before you sign means paying more than the headline price for months you cannot get back. Since 17 January 2025, Ofcom has required every network to state that rise in pounds and pence before you sign, so the number is there to check. Most people never do.

This is a ten-minute job, not a research project. Every figure below comes from a network's own pricing pages, dated, so you can check the rise before you commit rather than after the first bill lands.

## What Ofcom's pounds and pence rule means for you

Ofcom's rule bans a rise that is only ever a percentage or a promise to track inflation. Ofcom's own page on the change says any price rise written into a customer's contract from January 2025 needs to be set out in pounds and pence, prominently and transparently, at the point of sale, and that providers need to be clear about when any changes to prices will occur. A separate Ofcom statement confirms the ban itself: from 17 January 2025, phone, broadband and pay TV providers are prohibited from including inflation-linked or percentage-based price rise terms in new contracts.

That does not mean prices stop rising. It means the network has to tell you the figure, in pounds, before you agree to it, rather than tying it to a rate that only becomes clear later. The examples below are what each network states for plans on offer now. A plan you took out earlier may sit under different terms, so always check the figure on your own account rather than assuming it matches ours.

## Here is exactly what to do

1. Open the deal card above and read the price rise line. It shows the rise in pounds and pence, or "No price rise", for the exact plan on offer this week.
2. Check which month the rise lands. Several networks apply it in March, others in April, and the date matters if you are close to the end of a contract.
3. Multiply the monthly rise by the number of months left on the contract after that date lands, not the full term. That is the extra you will actually pay.
4. Add that figure to the total contract cost shown on the card. That total, not the first month's price, is what the deal actually costs you.
5. If a network states no price rise, such as giffgaff, Lebara or SMARTY above, you still want to check the plan is rolling or short enough that a future change does not surprise you, since a network without a mid-contract rise can still change its prices with notice between contracts.
6. Compare that adjusted total against another network's plan before you switch. [Our guide on SIM only versus contract](/blog/sim-only-vs-contract-which-is-cheaper/) uses the same total cost approach.

## Can I leave penalty free when the price rises?

Sometimes, and it depends on whether the rise matched what you were told. Ofcom's page on your rights says that if a price increase was clearly specified in your original contract in pounds and pence before you signed up, you do not automatically have the right to exit without penalty when that specific rise happens. What does give you an exit is if your provider raises the price by more than what your contract specified, or if the contract never specified an amount at all. In either of those cases, Ofcom says you are free to leave and move to another provider without being penalised. Ofcom also says your provider should tell you 30 days before a change that is not to your benefit takes effect, giving you time to act.

So reading the card before you sign matters more than checking your rights afterwards. Once a rise is stated in pounds and pence at the point of sale, which is now the rule, that rise alone will not usually let you leave early. Your leverage is knowing the number in advance and deciding whether the total, rise included, still beats the alternative.

## Do SIM only plans get a price rise too?

Yes on most of the networks we checked, no on a handful. O2's own pricing page states its airtime plan increase in pounds a month, and airtime is the SIM part of a phone contract as well as a SIM only plan. Vodafone and Three describe their rises the same way, applying to airtime and data plans rather than to devices alone. EE's own help page states the same rise applies to SIM only plans as to airtime plans generally, though as above, the amount depends on when you joined or last re-contracted.

iD Mobile is the exception worth knowing. Its price increases page says the fixed rise applies only if you upgrade or buy a new iD Mobile phone plan, and its SIM only deals page is headed with a no annual price rises line. So a SIM only customer on iD Mobile is protected from the fixed rise that a handset customer on the same network is not. giffgaff, Lebara and SMARTY go further and state no mid-contract rise across the board, whichever plan you are on. If you are choosing between SIM only and a handset contract for this reason alone, [our guide on SIM only versus contract](/blog/sim-only-vs-contract-which-is-cheaper/) sets out the rest of the comparison.

## Is the rise on the device part of my contract?

Not on the networks that publish a rise, with one exception. O2 states plainly that the cost of the device remains frozen while the airtime plan rises. Vodafone says its annual rise does not affect Device Plans. Three says the same, that its price increase does not apply to any device payments. In each case the rise sits on the airtime or data charge, not on what you are repaying for the handset itself.

iD Mobile again runs the other way. Its fixed annual increase is the one that lands on the phone plan, while SIM only stays outside it.
